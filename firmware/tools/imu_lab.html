      What the three sensors actually measure

      None of your three chips measures orientation. Each one measures something that orientation affects, and each one lies in its own way. The rest of this page is about combining them so the strengths of one cover the weaknesses of another.

      SensorMeasuresGood atFails when
      Gyroscope
      ICM-20600      		Angular rate ω around its x, y, z axes, in °/sFast, smooth, almost unaffected by vibration or magnetsAlways: a small constant bias integrates into an angle error that keeps growing
      Accelerometer
      ICM-20600      		Specific force: gravity pointing up, plus any acceleration of the board, in gAbsolute tilt (roll and pitch) that never driftsThe board accelerates (shaking, walking, a drone thrust). It can't tell gravity from motion. It is also blind to rotation around vertical.
      Magnetometer
      AK09918      		The magnetic field vector, in µT. Earth's field points north and dips into the ground.Absolute heading (yaw) that never driftsNearby iron, magnets, motors, laptop speakers, current-carrying wires. Needs calibration for the board's own magnetism.

      Your firmware already converts raw counts into physical units. At the ranges it sets, one count is:

      accel ±4 g → 8192 counts per g (ACCEL_CONFIG = 0x08) gyro ±2000 °/s → 16.4 counts per °/s (GYRO_CONFIG = 0x18) mag AK09918 → 0.15 µT per count (fixed)

      Pick the range for the fastest motion you will ever make, not the typical one. A flick of the wrist passes 1000 °/s. Past the limit the reading just sits at full scale, so the gyro reports less rotation than really happened, and that error stays in the orientation until gravity and the magnetometer slowly remove it. This board first ran at ±500 °/s, and fast turns past that limit are the most likely reason it came back misaligned after being handled. The firmware now flags every clipped sample and widens the ESKF's uncertainty, so it trusts gravity and the magnetometer right away. The cost of the wider range is coarser steps: 0.06 °/s per count instead of 0.015, still below the gyro's own noise.

      The key fact to hold on to: the gyro is good over short times and bad over long times, and the accelerometer and magnetometer are the opposite. Every algorithm below is a different way to act on that one sentence.

      Writing orientation down

      There are three common ways to store "which way the board is pointing", and the algorithms use all of them.

      Euler angles

      Three angles applied in order: yaw ψ around z, then pitch θ around y, then roll φ around x. Easy to read, which is why every card shows them. They break at pitch ±90°, where roll and yaw spin around the same axis and become ambiguous. That point is called gimbal lock.

      Quaternion

      Four numbers q = (w, x, y, z) with length 1. A rotation by angle α around unit axis u is q = (cos α/2, u·sin α/2). No singularities, cheap to multiply, easy to renormalise. Every filter here stores its state as a quaternion, except the Kalman filter.

      Conventions used throughout this page, and by Madgwick's and Mahony's reference code:

      • World frame NWU: x points to magnetic north, y to west, z up.
      • Sensor frame: the ICM-20600's axes printed on its package. Lying flat and still, the accelerometer reads (0, 0, +1) g.
      • q rotates sensor vectors into the world: vworld = q ⊗ vsensor ⊗ q*.
      • Signs: positive yaw turns the nose toward west (counter-clockwise seen from above). Positive pitch tips the nose down. Compass heading = −yaw.

      Converting a quaternion to the angles shown on the cards:

      roll φ = atan2( 2(wx + yz), 1 − 2(x² + y²) ) pitch θ = asin ( 2(wy − zx) ) ← asin saturates at ±90°: gimbal lock yaw ψ = atan2( 2(wz + xy), 1 − 2(y² + z²) )

      Gyro integration and drift

      If you know the orientation now and the rotation rate ω, you know the orientation one sample later. For quaternions the rate of change is

      q̇ = ½ · q ⊗ (0, ωx, ωy, ωz) ω in rad/s, sensor frame discrete, exact for constant ω over Δt: q[k+1] = q[k] ⊗ ( cos(|ω|Δt/2), ω/|ω| · sin(|ω|Δt/2) )

      This is the Gyro only card. It tracks fast motion perfectly, but it has two problems that no tuning can fix:

      • It doesn't know where it started. It only adds up changes. Press Reset filters and it stays at "level, facing north" forever.
      • Bias integrates into drift. A constant bias b becomes an angle error of b·t. 0.5 °/s sounds tiny, but it's 30° off after one minute. White noise also integrates, into a random walk that grows like √t.

      Try itThe board sits still with a 1.5 °/s gyro bias. Watch the Gyro only card turn slowly by itself while the others hold. Then open Calibrate and press "Measure gyro bias" to see the drift stop.

      Tilt from gravity, heading from the field

      When the board isn't accelerating, the accelerometer reads only gravity's reaction, the "up" vector seen from the sensor. Rotating (0, 0, 1) into the sensor frame gives a = (−sin θ, sin φ cos θ, cos φ cos θ), and solving backwards:

      roll φ = atan2( ay, az ) pitch θ = atan2( −ax, √(ay² + az²) )

      Gravity says nothing about yaw. Rotating the board around vertical leaves (0, 0, 1) unchanged. That's the magnetometer's job. Earth's field points north and dips into the ground, by about 50–60° in Europe. Un-tilt the measured field with φ and θ so it lies in the horizontal plane, then read its direction:

      hx = mx·cos θ + my·sin θ·sin φ + mz·sin θ·cos φ hy = my·cos φ − mz·sin φ yaw ψ = atan2( −hy, hx ) tilt-compensated compass

      This is the Accel + Mag card. It never drifts and finds the right answer instantly from any starting point. It is also jittery, because it passes on every bit of sensor noise. It tilts whenever you accelerate the board sideways, and it swings around when a magnet comes close.

      Try itAdd 0.6 g of shaking to the slow wobble. The Accel + Mag card shakes with it because it takes hand acceleration for tilt. The gyro-based filters barely notice.

      Try itBring a magnet close while the board sits still. The yaw of every filter that uses the magnetometer gets pulled away. The ones with slow gains get pulled less.

      Complementary filter

      The simplest fix: trust the gyro for fast changes and the accel/mag reference for slow ones. Each sample, propagate with the gyro, then nudge a small fraction of the way toward the absolute reference:

      q_pred = q ⊗ exp(ω·Δt / 2) gyro step q_ref = from_euler( φ_acc, θ_acc, ψ_mag ) absolute reference q = normalise( α·q_pred + (1 − α)·q_ref ) α = τ / (τ + Δt) crossover frequency f_c = 1 / (2π τ)

      That one line is a high-pass filter on the gyro plus a low-pass filter on the reference, and their sum is exactly 1 at every frequency. That's where the name "complementary" comes from. Below fc the result follows the accelerometer and magnetometer. Above it, the result follows the gyro.

      • τ small (0.05 s): follows the reference closely, so it's noisy and easily fooled by shaking.
      • τ large (5 s): smooth and resistant to shaking, but slow to correct gyro drift, and slow to recover after a reset.
      • At 100 Hz with τ = 0.5 s, α = 0.980. The classic "0.98" in hobby code is this same filter with τ = 0.5 s.

      Try itWobble with some shaking and a 1 °/s bias. Drag τ in Tune filters from 0.05 to 5 s and watch the Complementary line in the roll plot go from noisy to smooth, and the error number go through a minimum.

      Mahony: a PI controller on orientation

      Mahony's filter (2008) treats estimation as a feedback control problem. The "plant" is your current orientation estimate. The "sensor" is the measured gravity and field directions. The "actuator" is an extra rotation rate added to the gyro reading.

      1. Use the current estimate q to predict where gravity should appear in the sensor frame: v̂. Do the same for the magnetic field: ŵ.
      2. Compare with the measured, normalised a and m. The cross product of measured and predicted is a vector along the axis you'd rotate around to line them up, with length sin(angle between them). It's a rotation error you can feed back.
      3. Feed that error through a PI controller and add the result to the gyro rate.
      e = (a × v̂) + (m × ŵ) rotation error, sensor frame ∫e += Ki · e · Δt integral term ω_corr = ω_gyro + Kp · e + ∫e q = q ⊗ exp(ω_corr · Δt / 2)
      • Kp sets the loop bandwidth: roughly how many rad/s of correction per radian of error. It plays the same role as 1/τ in the complementary filter.
      • Ki is the interesting part. A constant gyro bias produces a constant error, and the integral grows until it cancels the bias exactly. So the integral term is a live gyro-bias estimate. Tune filters shows it.
      • Like any PI loop, too much Ki relative to Kp makes it oscillate.

      Try itTurntable spin with a 2 °/s bias and Ki = 0. Mahony settles with a steady error, because a P controller always needs some error to push back against a constant disturbance. Raise Ki to about 0.1. The bias estimate under Mahony in Tune filters settles at the simulated bias, about 1.2, −1.0 and 1.2 °/s on x, y and z (2 °/s in total), and the error drops to a fraction of a degree.

      Madgwick: gradient descent

      Madgwick's filter (2010) asks a different question: which quaternion would make the predicted gravity and field match the measurements best? It writes that as an error function f(q) and takes one step of gradient descent per sample.

      f(q) = [ q* ⊗ (0,0,0,1) ⊗ q − a ; q* ⊗ (0,bx,0,bz) ⊗ q − m ] ∇f = Jᵀ(q) · f(q) J = Jacobian of f q̇ = ½ q ⊗ (0, ω) − β · ∇f / |∇f| q += q̇ · Δt, then normalise

      The reference field (bx, 0, bz) is rebuilt every step from the measurement rotated into the world frame. That way the filter learns your local dip angle and never needs to know it in advance.

      • The gradient is normalised, so the correction is always β in size, no matter how big the error is. That's different from Mahony, whose correction grows with the error.
      • β has units of rad/s: the largest gyro error it can cancel. Madgwick suggests β = √(3/4)·(gyro error). 0.1 is a good start for most MEMS gyros.
      • Big β: fast convergence, but noisy and sensitive to shaking. Small β: smooth, but slow to recover. Press Reset filters and compare how long β = 0.03 and β = 0.3 take to find north.
      • This basic version has no integral term, so a constant gyro bias leaves a small steady error. Calibrate the bias first.

      Try itAll filters start at level and facing north, while the board actually points 30° west. Watch how quickly each one finds the true yaw.

      Kalman filter

      A Kalman filter tracks not just an estimate but how uncertain that estimate is, and uses the uncertainty to decide how much to trust each new measurement. The version here is the classic per-angle filter found in many hobby projects. It tracks two states for each of roll, pitch and yaw: the angle and the gyro bias.

      state x = [angle, bias] covariance P (2×2) predict angle += Δt · (ω − bias) P += Δt · (…) + Q uncertainty grows update y = z − angle z: accel angle or mag yaw S = P₀₀ + R K = [P₀₀, P₁₀] / S Kalman gain x += K · y P -= K · (row 0 of P) uncertainty shrinks
      • Q angle and Q bias say how much you expect the true angle and the bias to wander between samples (process noise).
      • R says how noisy the measurement z is. Only the ratio Q/R really matters: raising R makes the filter trust the gyro more, like raising τ.
      • For fixed Q and R, the gain K settles to a constant. A steady-state Kalman filter is then a complementary filter with a bias estimator, with its gains chosen from noise statistics instead of by hand.
      • The catch: this version runs three independent 1-D filters on Euler angles, feeding body-frame rates into world-frame angles. That's only right when the board is close to level. Near pitch ±90° it breaks down. The error-state filter in the next chapter fixes this.

      Try itLoop the loop: the board pitches all the way over. Every card's R and Y numbers jump as it passes vertical, because Euler angles break there. The quaternion filters (Complementary, Mahony, Madgwick) keep tracking the real orientation. The Kalman card loses it, and its error climbs past 20°.

      Error-state Kalman filter (ESKF)

      The per-angle Kalman filter put the Kalman machinery on the wrong quantity. Euler angles are large, coupled and singular. The ESKF splits the problem in two:

      • A nominal state: the quaternion q and gyro bias b. The gyro moves it forward exactly, with no approximation, the same way the Gyro only card does.
      • An error state δx = [δθ, δb]: the small rotation and bias error between the nominal state and the truth. This is the only thing the Kalman filter estimates.

      The error is always small, so the linear Kalman equations are accurate for it. It is also three plain angles, with no singularity and no unit-length constraint. After every measurement, the estimated error is folded into the nominal state and reset to zero.

      nominal true q = q ⊗ Exp(δθ), true bias = b + δb predict q ← q ⊗ Exp((ω − b)·Δt) Φ = [ I − [ω̂Δt]× −I·Δt ] P ← Φ P Φᵀ + Q [ 0 I ] Q = diag(σg²·Δt, σb²·Δt) gravity ĝ = Rᵀ·(0, 0, 1) predicted "up" in the sensor frame y = a/|a| − ĝ H = [ [ĝ]× 0 ] K = P Hᵀ (H P Hᵀ + σa²·I)⁻¹ δx = K·y, P ← (I − K H)·P heading z = atan2(m_world.y, m_world.x) H = [ −ĝᵀ 0 ] rotation about vertical only inject q ← q ⊗ Exp(δθ), b ← b + δb, δx ← 0

      Why this is the standard in drones and phones:

      • Bias comes for free. The −I·Δt block in Φ says "a bias error turns into an attitude error over time". The covariance P learns that link. When gravity reveals a tilt error, part of the correction goes into b.
      • It knows how sure it is. √P gives a 1-σ uncertainty per axis, shown in Tune filters. Turn off the magnetometer and watch yaw σ grow without limit: the filter knows heading is no longer observable.
      • Each sensor fixes only what it can measure. H for gravity, [ĝ]×, has no component along vertical, so the accelerometer never touches yaw. The heading update only touches yaw, so a magnet can never tilt the board.
      • New sensors are just another H. Barometer, GPS velocity or a camera each plug into the same predict/update loop.

      Your ESP32 runs this at 500 Hz (1 kHz with external I2C pull-ups) with a few extra rules. It skips the gravity update when |a| is more than 0.15 g away from 1 g, because the board is accelerating. It skips the heading update when the field strength changes by more than 15%, or the field's angle to gravity moves more than 6°, which means a magnet or iron nearby. Both references adapt over about 5 s, so a slowly approaching magnet can't drag them along. In the magnet test that gate cuts the ESKF's peak heading error from 48° to 16°. It predicts on every gyro sample and corrects with gravity at 100 Hz, using the mean of the accel samples since the last correction.

      Tuning: σg is the gyro noise density. The defaults, 0.01 °/s/√Hz and 0.0005 °/s/√s, come from a 2-hour Allan variance recording of this board (see the Allan variance chapter), raised about 3× to cover vibration and scale errors. σb is how fast the bias wanders. If the filter's σg is far from the real sensor's noise, it gets worse either way: try the Gyro noise slider in the Simulated board tab at 0.3 °/s and watch the ESKF error rise. σa and σψ are how much to trust gravity and heading. Lower σa means faster tilt correction but more sensitivity to motion that sneaks under the gate.

      Try itTurntable spin with a 2 °/s gyro bias and 0.4 g of shaking. Watch the ESKF's bias estimate settle on the true value, and its σ shrink, while the accel gate stops shaking from tilting it. Then untick Use magnetometer and watch yaw σ grow.

      Calibration

      Good calibration helps every algorithm more than any amount of tuning. Three calibrations matter, all in the Calibrate tab:

      Gyro bias

      Average the gyro for a few seconds while still and subtract it. Bias changes with temperature, so real devices redo this at every power-up, or keep estimating it online the way Mahony's integral term does.

      Hard iron

      Magnetised parts on the board add a constant vector to every reading. Rotating through all directions traces a sphere whose centre is shifted. The centre is the offset: (max + min) / 2 on each axis.

      Soft iron

      Nearby iron bends the field, stretching the sphere into an ellipsoid. Scaling each axis so its radius matches the average is a first-order fix. A full fit uses a 3×3 matrix.

      Axis alignment

      The two chips may not share axes. The angle between gravity and the field (the dip angle + 90°) is a property of where you are, not of how you hold the board. If it changes as you rotate, the axes are mapped wrong.

      Try itAggressive handling with a 35 µT hard-iron offset. Yaw estimates from every magnetometer-based filter wobble badly. Run the 20 s magnetometer calibration in the Calibrate tab and watch them settle.

      Allan variance: measuring your sensor's noise

      Every noise value in the filters so far was a guess. Allan variance measures them. Leave the board perfectly still for an hour or more, record the gyro, then ask one question for every averaging time τ: if I average the gyro over τ seconds, how much do neighbouring averages disagree?

      σ²(τ) = ½ · ⟨ (ȳₖ₊₁ − ȳₖ)² ⟩ ȳₖ = mean of the k-th chunk of length τ

      Short averages are dominated by white noise, which shrinks as you average longer. Very long averages get worse again, because the bias itself wanders. In between is the best you can ever do. Each noise process has its own slope on a log-log plot, so you can read them off one by one:

      SlopeNoiseRead itGoes into
      −1QuantizationVery short τ onlyUsually ignored
      −½White noise N (angle random walk)σ at τ = 1 s on the −½ lineESKF σ gyro, Kalman Q angle
      0Bias instability BCurve minimum ÷ 0.664How well bias can ever be known
      +½Rate random walk Kσ at τ = 3 s on the +½ lineESKF σ bias walk, Kalman Q bias, Mahony Ki
      +1Rate rampLong τUsually temperature drift

      Unit conversions people quote: N in °/s/√Hz × 60 = °/√h, and B in °/s × 3600 = °/h.

      Recording your own

      1. Leave the board powered for 15 minutes first, so its temperature settles. A warming chip shows up as a +1 slope.
      2. Put it on something that won't move: a heavy book on the floor works better than the desk you type on. Every bump of the table is a rotation the gyro will faithfully record.
      3. Close the lab's USB connection (Wi-Fi is fine) and run python tools/allan/allan_log.py --hours 2 from the firmware folder. It finds the ESP32's port and keeps the computer awake while recording.
      4. Afterwards run allan_analyze.py on the CSV. It prints N, B and K per axis, suggests ESKF settings, and writes the _adev.csv to load here.

      The longer you record, the further right the curve reaches. K is read from the longest averages, so it is the least certain number. With 2 hours, expect it to be within about a factor of 1.5.

      Closing the loop: a PID gimbal

      Estimating orientation is usually the first half of a job. The second half is control: using that estimate to drive a motor. Here your board is the base of a one-axis camera gimbal. A motor between base and platform tries to keep the platform level while the base rolls with the lab's board.

      The controller knows the motor's angle exactly from an encoder, but it only knows the base angle through the roll estimate of the filter you choose. So every estimation error turns directly into platform error. Feed it Accel + Mag and the platform jitters. Feed it Gyro only and it slowly tilts.

      platform angle (estimated) φ̂ = roll_est + θ_motor error e = setpoint − φ̂ torque u = Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt − Kd·dφ̂/dt clamped to motor limit platform dynamics φ̈ = u − c·(φ̇ − β̇) bearing friction drags it with the base
      • Kp stiffness: how hard it pushes back per degree of error. Too much and it overshoots and rings.
      • Kd damping: resists fast changes. It differentiates the estimate, so it amplifies estimator noise too.
      • Ki removes steady error against a constant push such as friction. Too much makes slow oscillations, and the integral is clamped to stop windup.

      Updating the firmware over Wi-Fi

      Once the board runs on a battery, USB is gone, so new firmware arrives over Wi-Fi instead (OTA, "over the air"). The 4 MB flash holds two firmware slots, ota_0 and ota_1. The running firmware receives the new image, writes it into the other slot, and checks it: format, chip type, project name and a SHA-256 hash. Only then does it tell the bootloader to start from the new slot. The old firmware stays untouched, so an upload cut off halfway changes nothing.

      A firmware that passes every check can still crash at startup. So a new image first boots as pending verify. If it has run for 10 s with its web server up, it marks itself good. If it resets before that, the bootloader goes back to the previous slot. That rule is why a bad update can't lock you out.

      1. Connect the PC to the board's Wi-Fi and run idf.py build.
      2. Run python tools/ota.py. It shows what the board runs now, uploads build/blink.bin (about 15 s), waits for the restart, and checks that the board's build id matches the file it sent.
      3. http://192.168.4.1/version in a browser shows the running version, build id, slot and state at any time.

      Changes to the partition table or the bootloader still need USB, because those live outside the app slots.

      Where to go next

      • Grow the ESKF: add accelerometer bias as three more states, or a barometer for altitude. Each new sensor needs only its own H and noise value.
      • Close the loop on the ESP32: run a PID like the gimbal chapter's on core 1, right after the filter, and drive a servo or motor with it.
      • Temperature compensation: the firmware already sends die temperature. Log bias against temperature and fit a line.
      • Magnetic declination: magnetic north isn't true north. Look up your local declination and add it to yaw if you need geographic headings.
